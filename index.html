Pet safety

Keep your dog safe around ponds

Dogs are the most common victims of toxic algae. They swim through scum, drink pond water, and lick it off their fur. Cyanobacteria poisoning can turn serious within an hour, and untreated cases in dogs are often fatal.

Before you go

  • Check the water with Blym and look at the map for recent reports.
  • Keep your dog leashed near water that is green, scummy, or smells bad.
  • Bring clean water so your dog isn't tempted to drink from the pond.

If your dog went in

  1. Rinse your dog with clean water right away, all the way down to the skin.
  2. Don't let your dog lick its fur or paws until it's rinsed.
  3. Call your vet or an emergency animal hospital and say "possible blue-green algae exposure."

Warning signs

If you see any of these after contact with pond water, it's an emergency. Go to a vet now.

Learn

Toxic algae, explained

What is a harmful algal bloom?

Cyanobacteria, often called blue-green algae, are tiny organisms that live in almost every lake and pond. When conditions are right they multiply fast and form a bloom. Some kinds make toxins: microcystin damages the liver, and anatoxin attacks the nervous system. A bloom that makes toxins is a harmful algal bloom, or HAB.

Why small Georgia lakes are at risk

Blooms grow in warm, calm, nutrient-rich water. Small neighborhood lakes and ponds are shallow, heat up fast, and collect fertilizer and pet waste from lawns around them. Georgia's long, hot summers and dry spells make it worse, and blooms can last into fall. EPA satellites only monitor about 2,000 of the largest U.S. lakes, so ponds like these depend on people noticing.

What blooms look like

  • Water that looks like spilled green paint or pea soup
  • Blue-green, bright green, or turquoise scum on the surface
  • Streaks or swirls of color, often piled up along the shore by wind
  • A rotten, musty, or grassy smell

Bloom or look-alike?

Possible bloom
Paint-like or pea-soup water, surface scum, blue-green streaks
Usually harmless
Yellow pollen dust in spring, duckweed (tiny separate leaves), stringy green mats you can lift with a stick, muddy water

The stick test: dip a stick in. If it comes out looking like it was dipped in paint, treat it as a bloom. If stringy strands hang off it, it's probably filamentous algae. When you aren't sure, treat it as a bloom.

Common myths

  • "Boiling the water makes it safe." No. Boiling doesn't destroy algal toxins and can concentrate them.
  • "Clear water means no toxins." Not always. Toxins can stay in the water after a bloom fades.
  • "It's only dangerous if you drink it." Skin contact and breathing in spray can also make people sick.

How to prevent blooms

  • Use less lawn fertilizer, especially near water and before rain.
  • Pick up after pets so waste doesn't wash into the pond.
  • Leave a strip of tall plants along the shore to catch runoff.
  • Report blooms so agencies know where to test.

Sources: Georgia EPD, CDC, EPA, and USGS guidance on harmful algal blooms.

About

Why I built Blym

I live on a small lake in Gwinnett County, Georgia. Toxic algae blooms can sicken people and kill dogs, but EPA satellites only watch the largest lakes, so lakes like mine have no one checking. Existing apps let you report a bloom once you already know it's one. Blym helps the person standing at the water figure out whether it's dangerous, what to do right now, and then share it with neighbors.
